Last Contango in Paris

When the collateral needs four more years, bankruptcy is the one thing it cannot survive

Writing Sample | Aug 17th 2026

Paris, Kentucky, the seat of Bourbon County, is learning that whiskey is a surprisingly illiquid asset. A barrel that might be worth a fortune in eight years is worth almost nothing to a creditor who must move it today when the barrel is used as collateral for a loan. Barrel-backed loans are the heart of the Kentucky industry. To keep up with the huge demand for spirits during Covid, distillers grew their inventory from 6m ageing barrels in 2012 to 16m in 2025. However, now there is a global oversupply of spirits and not enough people wanting to drink them, causing the barrels to suddenly lose their value until they can be sold for a future price to cover costs and prevent bankruptcy, the classic risky Contango trade known the world over in the oil markets.

The lesson travels well beyond the rickhouse, because the same defect sits inside the loans now written against subprime car buyers and against the graphics chips that power artificial intelligence.

Omens appear to be filling up. In August a heritage bourbon brand, Chicken Cock, first bottled in 1856, was folded into a North Carolina drinks group on undisclosed terms.1 Trading the label is what an industry does when the liquid behind it stops moving. And Paris has two larger cautionary tales in view: the receivership of Uncle Nearest, in Tennessee, and the closure of an Alabama cooperage that once fed the Jack Daniel’s distillery. Beneath both lies a dangerous number. Kentucky is sitting on a record stock of ageing bourbon,2 upward of three barrels for every resident of the state, an inventory laid down on the confident assumption that a decade of double-digit growth was a permanent condition rather than a short-term Covid trend.

Uncle Nearest, the fast-growing distiller founded by Fawn Weaver in 2017, was placed into receivership in August 2025 after defaulting on loans exceeding $100m from Farm Credit Mid-America.3 Farm Credit is an agricultural co-operative, not a private-credit fund, and that is precisely the point: if a lender this conservative mispriced barrel collateral, the risk is larger still at the private-credit firms now racing into asset-based finance. Apollo, Ares, Blackstone and Fortress have built specialty-finance platforms lending against receivables, equipment and other hard-to-sell assets, the fastest-growing corner of a market past $2trn.4 Farm Credit’s central complaint concerned the collateral itself: an inspection the previous autumn found discrepancies between the barrel inventory the company had certified and what could be verified.5 The Weavers deny wrongdoing, blaming a former finance chief. What is not in dispute is what happened when the receiver tried to sell. He approached more than a hundred parties and drew a single tentative reply;6 an earlier attempt to move 10,000 casks at $1,000 apiece produced one bid, for a tenth of them, at $400.7 Most telling of all, Farm Credit chose to keep funding the company’s losses rather than seize its security, even paying $1.09m to clear a lien that sat ahead of it on the barrels.6 When a secured creditor pays to avoid collecting, the collateral is no longer collateral. It is a hostage.

The distress moves backwards down the supply chain to the people who make the containers. On July 16th Independent Stave Company told Alabama it would close its Trinity cooperage, once a Brown-Forman plant turning out some 1,300 barrels a day, on September 14th, with 71 jobs lost.8 These are not diversified economies. They are single-employer towns with a tax abatement and a workforce trained to do one thing. The retrenchment has been methodical: 112 jobs cut at a Kentucky cooperage in 2025, a Louisville plant bought from Brown-Forman for $13.66m in June, and now Alabama.8,9

Demand for new barrels has collapsed alongside the value of the old ones, for three reinforcing reasons. Tariff retaliation has shut export markets: American whiskey shipments to the European Union fell 35% in 2025, with a suspended 30% tariff due to return in August.10 Tastes are changing: roughly one in nine American adults now takes a GLP-1 weight-loss drug, up from one in thirty-three two years earlier, and American alcohol volumes fell about 5% in 2025 as the share of adults who drink slipped from 62% to 54%.11 And the resale market for the barrel itself has caved: a used ex-bourbon cask, prized by Scotch and Irish distillers, has fallen from around $350 in 2022 to roughly $90 as their own demand softens.12

Wider concerns in esoteric collateral

Collateral is never worth its sticker price in distress, because seizing an asset and selling it is a market-making activity nobody wants to perform, and a sector-wide downturn floods the market with identical goods at the very moment their value is called upon. The buyers who might absorb them are the same firms already drowning in their own. The market for the collateral and the market for the borrower’s product are one market, and they fail together.

Consider Tricolor, a Dallas subprime car lender that filed for bankruptcy in September 2025. Prosecutors allege its executives double-pledged some $800m of collateral, using the same auto loans to secure several banks at once; a later review found roughly 29,000 of 70,000 active loans shared attributes, including vehicle identification numbers, with others.13 JPMorgan Chase and Fifth Third disclosed losses above $340m.13 A car at least has a title registry and a liquid auction, and even there verification failed while subprime borrowers were falling delinquent at rates unseen in three decades.14 Now transpose the problem to graphics processors. CoreWeave’s $8.5bn facility secured on Nvidia chips carries an investment-grade rating,15 yet the borrower depreciates those chips over six years while engineers reckon their useful life closer to three, and rental rates for the workhorse H100 have fallen by as much as 90% from their peak.16 A barrel loses value only if sold early; a chip loses it regardless. Both, in a default, leave the lender asking the one question: what will this fetch today if I have to liquidate it when everyone else is doing so?

Scepticism should cut both ways. The losses have been absorbed without a wider spread to the spirits market, and much of the whiskey pain is simply overproduction being purged, which is how gluts end. It would be wrong to mistake this inventory correction for a credit crisis. But the common thread is neither cyclical nor confined to spirits: the verification of collateral has been outsourced to the borrower, and its value modelled on an orderly market that will not exist when it is needed. Both assumptions hold beautifully until they are tested, together. Regulators have warned about the opacity of private-credit valuations, and the industry is now conceding the point.17 After a spike in redemption requests forced Blue Owl and Apollo to restrict withdrawals from flagship funds, Apollo pledged to publish daily marks on its credit book.18 The barrels simply make visible what a model price otherwise hides. Chicken Cock’s new owner bought a brand, a story and two tasting rooms, not a warehouse of maturing stock, because the stock is the part no one wants. In Paris the barrels are still ageing, still notionally worth more each quarter, still marked somewhere as security. That is the wager beneath every barrel-backed loan, and beneath a great deal else now financed the same way: that the future price arrives before the creditor does. Contango rewards only those who can afford to wait. The barrels can. Their owners may not. ■
