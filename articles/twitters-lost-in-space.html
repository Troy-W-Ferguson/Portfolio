Twitter’s Lost in Space

Public shareholders now own the debt from Elon Musk’s worst deal. The rocket company is the wrapper.

Writing Sample | Tue 18th August 2026

For more than a decade, Elon Musk told anyone who asked that SpaceX would not go public until Mars was secure. On June 12th he took it public anyway, in the largest initial offering in history — roughly $75bn raised at a valuation near $1.77trn, listing on the Nasdaq under the ticker SPCX.1

The stock has since done what over-subscribed mega-listings do. It closed at a record $211.39 on June 16th, broke its $135 offer price on July 15th, bottomed at $104.83 on August 3rd and stands at $146.23 today.23 A 911.5m-share unlock on August 6th produced a violent round trip: the stock fell hard into it, then rallied when the feared supply never came.4 A second tranche, up to 319m shares, lands on August 20th.5

That volatility is the ordinary business of a newly floated mega-cap. The more interesting question is what the float contains. The entity that listed in June is not the rocket company Musk described in 2013. Over sixteen months, through two mergers he controlled on both sides, SpaceX absorbed the social network formerly known as Twitter, the AI company built on top of it, and the debt attached to both.

The Nevada two-step

Musk bought Twitter in October 2022 for roughly $44bn, financed with about $13bn of acquisition debt banks then struggled for years to syndicate. In March 2025 X was folded into xAI at $45bn including some $12bn of debt — $1bn above the take-private price, which let Musk tell Twitter co-investors they had been made whole.6 X thereafter ceased to exist as a separately observable business.

Then, on February 2nd 2026, SpaceX acquired X.AI Holdings in an all-stock deal valuing xAI at $250bn and SpaceX at $1trn — the largest corporate combination on record.7 Reuters reported, citing people familiar with the transaction, that it ran through two intermediary companies incorporated in Nevada: a structure that let SpaceX acquire xAI without triggering the smaller company’s debt covenants, and therefore without any requirement to repay bondholders. xAI was retained as a wholly owned subsidiary rather than integrated, insulating the parent from its legal liabilities.8

None of this is improper; tax specialists treat it as a textbook reverse triangular reorganisation.9 But be precise about what it accomplished. Debt raised against a declining social-media business, at rates reflecting that decline, moved onto the balance sheet of the most valuable private company on earth without the creditors getting a vote. They noticed: xAI’s $3bn of five-year notes, issued at a 12.5% yield, traded at 107 cents on the dollar before the deal and 113.5 within days.8 Four months later, that balance sheet was sold to the public.

What the shareholders bought

The three-segment structure — Space, Connectivity, AI — is not a diversification story. It is a cross-subsidy. Connectivity produced $11.39bn of 2025 revenue, 61% of sales, and was the only profitable segment, earning $4.42bn. In the second quarter it made $1.66bn of operating income on $4.29bn of revenue; Space lost $542m and AI lost $1.26bn.10

The quarter was otherwise strong: revenue up 92% to $7.81bn, adjusted EBITDA up 191% to $3.54bn, net loss narrowed to $541m from $1.01bn.11 The company is not profitable. It is losing money at a decreasing rate on rapidly growing revenue — the profile investors have rewarded in every software company of the past decade and almost no capital-intensive one. Capital expenditure was $18.4bn in the quarter, against $10.1bn in the prior three months.10 Analyst targets run from $62 to $800, averaging near $227.2 The dispersion is the tell. A $62-to-$800 range on a $1.8trn company is not a forecast. It is an admission that nobody knows.

The profit engine is a treadmill

Starlink’s economics rest on an asset base that expires. Low-earth-orbit satellites have finite lives and must be continuously replaced, so the segment’s margin carries a replenishment line that never goes away and grows with the constellation. This is not a network that gets built once and harvested; it must be rebuilt, forever, at a rate proportional to its size.

That is manageable while SpaceX owns the cheapest launch capability on earth. But because it launches its own satellites, replenishment cost is largely internal and the transfer price attached to it is a management choice — the reported margin of Connectivity and the reported loss of Space are partly an artefact of allocation investors cannot test. And pricing power is already softening: revenue per Starlink subscriber fell 22% year-on-year as the company pushed into international markets and cheaper tiers, even as subscribers doubled to 12m.12

Nobody can outvote him

Musk holds 6.42bn shares — 48.4% of the economics, but 82.4% of the votes, through Class B stock carrying ten votes apiece; the stake was worth more than $900bn when disclosed this month.13 Public shareholders hold economic exposure with no meaningful governance mechanism, in a company whose recent history is a sequence of related-party transactions in which Musk set the valuation on both sides. The pattern is old: difficult assets sell more easily pooled with good ones, acquiring a multiple they could never have earned alone. There is no reason to assume bad faith. There is every reason to note that the structure provides no check if judgment fails.

One undemonstrated capability

Starship has flown thirteen integrated tests, most recently on July 24th. No two Starships have yet met in orbit, docked and passed propellant between them — a demonstration NASA’s inspector general reported in March had already slipped a year.14 The Artemis lander needs up to twelve such transfers in sequence to land and return; NASA’s administrator says he is confident of a 2027 landing, while SpaceX’s internal documents point to September 2028.1516 The IPO filing concedes that Starship delays would impede next-generation satellites, satellite-to-mobile connectivity and in-orbit compute alike.17 Three growth narratives route through one undemonstrated vehicle.

The Iridium problem

Motorola spent more than $5bn on 66 satellites that worked exactly as designed.18 By August 1999 Iridium had roughly 20,000 subscribers against projections near five million, breached its covenants and filed for Chapter 11.19 In November 2000 a group led by Dan Colussy bought the whole system out of bankruptcy for about $25m — half a cent for every dollar Motorola had spent — and, selling to militaries and shipping rather than business travellers, had it profitable by 2005.20 Kellogg titled its case study “Lost in Space?”21

SpaceX is not Iridium; 12m paying subscribers and a dominant launch franchise are things Iridium never had. The lesson is narrower. An extraordinary orbital asset can be worth a rounding error to its creditors while remaining perfectly functional, because a constellation is worth what it can be made to earn, not what it cost to put up. Motorola built the thing. Somebody else made the money.

What to watch

August 20th is the near-term event. Beyond it, three things decide whether SPCX at $146 looks cheap in two years: whether Starlink’s subscriber growth keeps outrunning replenishment capex now revenue per user is falling; whether Starship demonstrates orbital propellant transfer; and whether the AI segment’s burn stabilises as a share of revenue. Nvidia, which supplies the chips, disclosed a $20.97bn stake this month — the sort of circular arrangement markets have been eager to fund and slow to scrutinise.22

For a decade Musk said the company would not list until Mars was secure. Mars is not secure. What was secured, in June, was the debt raised against a social network, a chatbot and a fleet of GPUs — repackaged and sold to the public inside the best hardware business of the century. That may work out fine. It usually does, right up until it doesn’t. ■
